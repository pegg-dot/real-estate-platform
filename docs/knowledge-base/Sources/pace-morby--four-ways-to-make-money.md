# Pace Morby — The Basics of Real Estate (Four Ways to Make Money)

**Source type:** Podcast / training session (Sub2 community, "Subto")
**Speaker:** Pace Morby — creative-finance investor, founder of the Sub2 community
**Captured:** 2026-05-31
**One-line:** A ground-floor map of how money is actually made in real estate, why
"creative finance" beats traditional cash deals on most motivated-seller situations,
and how to figure out what *you specifically* should do.

> Note on bias: Pace sells a paid community ("Sub2," ~$10k to join). A lot of the talk
> is also a pitch for *why a community beats a single-strategy mentorship*. The
> frameworks below are still solid; just read the "join my community" parts as marketing.

---

## 1. The four ways to make money in real estate

Everything fits under four umbrellas. The first three are **investor** roles; the
fourth is a **support** role.

| Umbrella | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Wholesale** | Get a property under contract, sell the contract — you never own it | Bird dogging, DTS/DTA/DTR wholesaling, "orbit" |
| **Develop** | Add value through construction | Fix & flip, build new, tear down & rebuild, add units (e.g. expand an RV park) |
| **Buy & Hold** | Own the asset, generate income | (huge list — see §2) |
| **Service** | Support the transaction, don't own the deal | Agent, transaction coordinator (TC), gator, title company, contractor, CPA, bookkeeper, videographer, bank |

Key reframe: when you meet anyone in real estate, mentally bucket them —
*are you a wholesaler, a developer, a holder, or a service provider?*

---

## 2. Buy & Hold is enormous — and these are "exit strategies," not deal sources

Pace stresses that things like "Section 8" or "Airbnb" are **exit strategies** — they
describe *how you make money on a property once you own it*, NOT how you found or
financed it. Saying "I'm a Section 8 investor" tells you nothing about where the deal
came from. This is a recurring trap in single-strategy mentorships: they teach the
exit but never teach you how to *find* the deal.

Buy-and-hold sub-strategies mentioned:
- **Long-term rental (LTR)** — standard 12–36 month leases. Easiest; lowest turnover.
- **Short-term rental (STR)** — Airbnb.
- **Mid-term rental (MTR)** — leasing to insurance companies (displaced homeowners
  after a fire/flood), corporate relocations. Higher rent for shorter duration +
  turnover hassle.
- **Section 8** — government pays a portion of rent.
- **HUDVASH** — Section 8 for veterans.
- **Co-living** — rent by the room.
- **Assisted living / sober living / behavioral & mental health / dementia care** —
  higher-touch, operationally heavy (Pace owned 6 sober-living facilities, sold them
  all — hated the 2am "someone died" calls; says teams make it viable but it's not
  for beginners or the team-averse).
- **Battered women's shelters** — government-supported niche.
- **RV parks / mobile home parks** — Pace's favorite; ~$3–5M deals netting $20–30k/mo.
- **Multifamily / apartments** — and you can layer exit strategies on top (e.g. a
  "super Section 8" 12-unit where 100% of tenants are Section 8).
- **Land banking** — buy land in the "path of progress" and hold 5–15 years for the
  city to grow to it. High patience, long reward; NOT a beginner play.

## 3. "New investor" is the wrong question — ask the "you-investor" question

The single most important mental model in the talk.

- **Wrong question:** *"What should a new investor focus on?"* → unanswerable, because
  the right answer depends entirely on the person.
- **Right question:** *"How does an investor know what they should focus on?"*

The answer depends on **your situation**, specifically two axes:
1. **Time vs. money** — *"I have no money" → you'd better have time* (drive for
   dollars, door knock, bird dog). *"I have no time" → you'd better have money* (pay
   for inbound leads / marketing).
2. **Urgency / risk tolerance** — homeless-and-broke needs a *quick win* (wholesale,
   bird dog, orbit). Someone with a stable good job should *not* blow it up to
   wholesale — they should carve out 6–12 hrs/week to acquire buy-and-hold assets that
   eventually replace their income.

> Worked example from the talk: to someone with a solid W-2 job who likes it, Pace's
> advice is "keep the job, spend a few hours a week buying RV parks / rentals, grow the
> portfolio 2–3x over a year, and within ~24 months you never worry about money again."
> He explicitly would *not* tell that person to become a wholesaler.

**Relevance to Nate:** stable income + family-trust capital + long horizon = you are
much closer to the "buy-and-hold / forever-money" profile than the "wholesale / today-
money" profile. Don't let wholesale-heavy content pull you off that.

## 4. Today money / Tomorrow money / Forever money

A simple way to classify every strategy by *when it pays*:

- **Today money** — active income now: wholesaling, bird dogging, gator lending,
  being an agent, fix & flip (faster cash). Transactional.
- **Tomorrow money** — medium-term payoff: development, BRRRR-style projects.
- **Forever money** — the goal for most: buy-and-hold portfolios, multifamily, RV
  parks. The "forever money" everyone actually wants.

Lending (being a "gator") is notable: it's today-money you can do *forever*.
Wholesaling and being an agent are the "9-to-5 of real estate" — good income, but
you're still a service provider, not an investor, and you don't want to do them forever.

## 5. Where deals come from: every deal starts with a motivated/pained seller

100% of deals originate from a **seller** with a reason to sell. The acquisition
channel is one of three:

- **DTS — Direct to Seller** (call/door-knock/mail the owner directly; off-market)
- **DTA — Direct to Agent** (call the listing agent on stale listings; term Pace
  coined)
- **DTR — Direct to Referral** (probate attorneys, tax attorneys referring families)

On-market listings (Zillow, Crexi, LoopNet) are **agent-controlled** — don't call the
seller directly; the listing agreement means the agent gets paid regardless, and the
seller will just redirect you. (Full detail on lists, tools, and costs lives in
`../Concepts/lead-generation.md`.)

## 6. The "Bunnies" — the core lesson of the whole talk

> *"It's never about the house. It's about the bunnies."*

"Bunnies" = the **emotional, underlying reason** a seller actually needs to sell.
Comes from Pace's first deal (Janie, a retiring teacher): 30+ investors offered on
her house; she sold to Pace — below a competing offer — because he was the only one
who asked how he could *help* and actually solved her real problem (three giant
Flemish rabbits she couldn't rehome). His mom took the rabbits; Janie sold him the
house and trusted his number sight-unseen.

The Sub2 logo is bunny ears. The operating principle:
- You don't "negotiate." You **find the bunnies and bring a solution to them.**
- Examples of bunnies: foreclosure, probate, divorce, death in the family, job
  relocation, retirement, capital-gains exposure, landlord burnout.
- "I just want to sell my house" = no bunnies = not motivated = you can't add value.
- When you feel resistance on a call, you're "still in the living room and haven't
  gotten to the backyard yet" — i.e., you haven't built enough rapport for them to
  reveal the real motivation.

**Why Pace was good at it:** prior background — 2 years door-knocking on a mission
(79,800 doors) + 10 years as a contractor selling to homeowners in their living rooms.
Wholesaling was the same skill: marketing → living room → rapport → listen → offer →
follow up → close.

## 7. Creative finance vs. traditional/cash — and the equity reality

This is the central thesis. (Deep version in `../Concepts/creative-finance.md`.)

- **Traditional / cash** = a zero-sum game. Your discount comes straight out of the
  seller's pocket. Cash investors generally buy around **~50¢ on the dollar**, which
  most sellers won't accept — so cash can't solve most situations.
- **Creative finance** (sub2, seller finance, hybrids, Morby method, lease options,
  ~26 strategies) is the only structure where the seller wins *more* as you win.

**The equity trap (critical):** "equity" is NOT (Zillow value − loan balance). It's
what's left *after* the ~10% cost to sell (agent commissions both sides, title,
inspection credits, lender concessions). So a seller who "owes 381k on a 400k house"
actually has **negative** equity — selling traditionally, they'd write a check
(~$21k) to get rid of it.

- Pace's stats from the talk: ~6M US homeowners hold 2–4% rate mortgages with little/no
  equity; ~20%+ of sellers currently *write a check* to sell; ~900/month in Maricopa
  County alone.
- This is exactly the **Subject-To (sub2)** opportunity: take over the existing
  low-rate mortgage with no money down. From the seller's view you "gave them ~$21k"
  by saving them the check they'd otherwise write.

**The two-word rule of thumb:**
- **NEED → Subject-To.** Seller has no equity / is in distress; sub2 is the only thing
  that works.
- **GREED → Seller Finance.** Seller has equity and wants top dollar (and to dodge
  capital gains); seller financing gets them a higher price + monthly payments + tax
  deferral, while you skip the bank.

**Capital-gains angle:** an older seller selling a long-held, appreciated asset faces
a large cap-gains hit on a cash sale. Seller financing spreads the gain over years —
a genuine win you can offer that cash buyers cannot. (Used on the 161-unit and RV-park
examples below.)

## 8. Proper wholesaling (and why it's being regulated)

- **Right way ("buyer-first"):** before you contract a seller, ask a real buyer what
  they'd pay sight-unseen; contract *below* that; the spread is your fee. You never
  tell the buyer your contract price — just ask what they'll pay.
- **Worked example:** seller will sell at $250k; buyer (Jamil) will pay $400k. Pace
  contracts at 250, assigns to Jamil at 400, keeps $150k. Jamil resells at 450, keeps
  50. Seller happy at 250.
- **Wrong way ("reverse wholesaling" as commonly done):** contract a seller with *no*
  real buyer lined up, then scramble — and when no buyer materializes, ghost the
  seller. This is what's getting wholesaling **outlawed/licensed in ~11 states.** A
  classic "I have a contract that won't sell, anyone know buyers?" DM is the tell that
  someone contracted too high with no buyer.

## 9. Deal economics — real numbers from the talk

These illustrate that headline spreads are *not* profit.

- **The $232k flip:** bought at $232k, listing at ~$425k → nets only **~$30k.** Why:
  the seller actually contracted at $207k (bird dog Sandra got $5k, the DTS Sub2
  wholesaler got $20k → $232k to Pace), then renovation + hard-money interest +
  private-money interest + closing costs + the ~10% cost to sell eat the rest.
- **161-unit apartment, Tucson (creative/seller finance):** listed $20M, drifting down
  on lowball cash offers. Pace offered **$19.5M with seller financing** — $5M down
  (raised from Sub2 students, who own 70% of the deal for ~20% of the cash), $14.5M
  seller-financed at 4% for 15 yrs. Gross rents ~$165k/mo; ~$46k/mo to the seller +
  opex → **nets ~$80k/mo.** No bank, no credit check, no W-2s.
- **First month as a wholesaler:** $5k on postcards (via Yellow Letters HQ — he says
  don't use them) → ~20 inbound calls (~$400/call then; ~$2,000/call in Phoenix now) →
  5 contracts, 3 closed → ~$50k. The other ~50% of leads he *couldn't* close because
  they wanted too much money or had no equity — which is what pushed him to learn
  creative finance in his first 30 days (taught by a title rep, Eene Brown).

## 10. The "community as an economy" pitch (read as marketing, but the logic is real)

Pace's argument for why a multi-strategy community beats a single-strategy mentorship:
a real deal needs many roles — a finder (wholesaler/bird dog), money (gator/lender),
paperwork (TC), title, contractor, property manager. A community supplies all of them
to each other, so a member can assemble a full deal from inside the network (~380 deals
reportedly posted daily in their group). The takeaway worth keeping regardless of
whether you join anything: **think in terms of the full cast a deal requires, and build
relationships across all of those roles.**
